Measurable Learning Objectives

By the end of the sequence, learners will be able to:

  • Write a prompt that names a purpose, audience, subject, visual constraints, and at least one accessibility consideration, then explain how each choice supports the intended message.
  • Generate and compare at least three image variants, recording the prompt, tool, date, and changes made between iterations in a process log.
  • Verify at least two factual or contextual claims in a selected image against reliable sources and label any unsupported or uncertain details.
  • Identify at least two representation or bias risks in AI-generated imagery and propose a specific revision or alternative approach.
  • Produce a final educational image with a source and licence note, an AI-use disclosure, and a short human-created explanation of the design decisions.
  • Give and use peer feedback against the project rubric, making at least one documented improvement before submission.

Structured Pedagogical Project: Visual Evidence Story

Project challenge

In small teams, learners create one educational image and a short caption that explain a curriculum concept for a defined audience. The submission must include a prompt-and-process log, at least two verified factual claims, an AI-use disclosure, a source and licence note, and an accessibility description. AI imagery is treated as a draft to be questioned and revised, not as automatic evidence.

Duration

Four 60-minute sessions plus a 30-minute showcase. Teachers may run each session as two shorter workshops.

Group size

Teams of three or four, with individual process logs and reflection.

Final product

One accessible educational image, a 100â€“150 word caption, a process log, and a source and licence record.

Materials and preparation

  • Teacher-approved image-generation tool or a teacher-prepared set of sample outputs; devices and a shared display.
  • Project brief, prompt planner, process-log template, source-verification checklist, accessibility checklist, and assessment rubric.
  • Reliable subject sources and reference images that learners own, have licensed, or can legally reuse.
  • An offline or low-connectivity alternative: printed reference images, paper storyboards, and a non-generative digital collage activity.
  • Before class, check the tool's age requirements, account settings, data-retention terms, accessibility features, and school policy.

Team roles

Researcher: selects and checks sources. Prompt designer: drafts and revises prompts. Visual editor: makes human edits and checks composition. Verification and accessibility lead: records uncertainties, writes alt text, and checks the rubric. Rotate roles so that technical control and decision-making are shared.

Sequence, timing, and steps

  1. Session 1 â€” Frame the problem (60 min): analyse a teacher-selected image, identify its purpose and audience, discuss risks, choose a curriculum concept, and write a source-backed creative brief. Output: brief and source list.
  2. Session 2 â€” Prompt, generate, and compare (60 min): draft a prompt with subject, context, constraints, and accessibility requirements; generate at least three variants; record tool, date, prompt, and observations; select one candidate without assuming it is accurate. Output: comparison table and process log.
  3. Session 3 â€” Verify, revise, and make accessible (60 min): verify at least two claims with reliable sources, mark unsupported details, revise the prompt or edit the image by hand, test the image with the accessibility checklist, and write alt text. Output: revised image, verification notes, and accessibility description.
  4. Session 4 â€” Compose, cite, and peer review (60 min): add a concise caption, AI-use disclosure, source and licence note, and a 100â€“150 word design rationale; exchange feedback using the rubric and make one documented improvement. Output: submission ready for showcase.
  5. Showcase (30 min): present the image and explain one human decision, one verified claim, one limitation, and one next revision. Peers ask evidence-based questions.

Differentiation

  • Support: provide a prompt frame, visual vocabulary bank, worked example, paired tool operation, sentence starters, and additional time.
  • Extension: compare two tools or models, test a constrained brief, create a provenance ledger, or remix only assets with confirmed licences.
  • Language and communication: allow bilingual planning, oral rehearsal, labelled sketches, and a spoken or recorded rationale.
  • Participation: offer a non-generative image-analysis pathway and let learners contribute through research, scripting, editing, accessibility testing, or presentation.

Assessment

Criterion Evidence of success
Subject accuracy At least two claims are supported by reliable sources; uncertainties are labelled rather than hidden.
Visual communication The image and caption are appropriate for the stated audience and purpose; composition choices are explained.
Responsible AI process The log records prompts, iterations, tool use, human edits, disclosure, sources, and licence status.
Representation and accessibility The team identifies a bias risk, takes a concrete response, and supplies usable alt text or an equivalent description.
Collaboration and reflection Roles are shared, peer feedback is used, and the learner identifies one limitation and one specific revision.

Inclusion and Accessibility

Plan for access before the tool is opened. Offer a text-first brief, a paper storyboard, or a teacher-prepared image set for learners who cannot or do not wish to use a generative service. Allow oral, signed, written, or recorded responses, and let learners choose a role that values research, scripting, editing, accessibility testing, or presentation as well as prompt writing.

Accessibility checklist for the final image

  • Provide concise alt text and a longer description when the image conveys complex information.
  • Do not communicate meaning through color alone; check contrast, text size, and reading order.
  • Use a keyboard-accessible, screen-reader-compatible workflow where possible and provide captions or transcripts for any accompanying audio or video.
  • Avoid flashing, overwhelming motion, or unnecessary visual clutter; test the output at different zoom levels.
  • Do not require a learner to disclose a disability, identity, trauma, or personal experience to participate.
  • Invite learners to flag harmful or inaccessible content and provide a clear route to request a revision or alternative format.

Data Protection and GDPR

  • Use only institution-approved accounts and tools. Do not enter names, faces, contact details, precise locations, health information, ethnicity, biometric data, or other personal or sensitive information into prompts or uploads.
  • Use fictional, aggregated, or properly anonymised examples. Obtain the required consent or parental permission before sharing identifiable learner work, and never publish a student's image or name without the institution's approval process.
  • Minimise data: upload only what the task needs, disable public sharing where possible, and delete working files when they are no longer required under school policy.
  • Review the provider's privacy notice, retention period, deletion options, model-training settings, subprocessors, and international-transfer information before use. Record the date and version checked because terms can change.
  • Follow the institution's lawful-basis, safeguarding, records, and data-breach procedures. Consult the school's data protection officer when a task involves children, sensitive data, or a new provider.

Copyright, Licences, and Attribution

An AI-generated image is not automatically copyright-free, royalty-free, public domain, or cleared for reuse. Rights and permitted uses depend on the tool's current terms, the input materials, the output, the jurisdiction, and the intended audience. Treat this section as a classroom checklist, not legal advice.

  • Start with images, textures, fonts, and references that learners own, have licensed, or can verify as public domain or openly licensed. Record creator, title, source URL, licence, access date, and modifications.
  • Do not assume that a prompt, a style name, a living artist's name, a character, a logo, or a trademark can be copied. Avoid requests intended to imitate a identifiable artist or protected work; use descriptive visual qualities instead.
  • Check the provider's terms for ownership, attribution, commercial use, redistribution, and educational display. A tool's marketing language is not a substitute for the current licence or terms of service.
  • Include an AI-use disclosure and a source and licence note with the final image. Seek permission when the intended reuse is uncertain, and follow local copyright exceptions or fair-dealing guidance only after institutional review.

Representation, Bias, and Human Verification

Generative systems can reproduce stereotypes, omit communities, invent details, or present a plausible image as factual evidence. Human judgement remains responsible for the final educational claim.

Representation audit

Ask who is present, absent, central, marginalised, or reduced to a stereotype. Check gender, race and ethnicity, disability, age, body type, language, culture, religion, and socioeconomic context without treating any identity as a visual shortcut.

Human verification

Check factual details against at least two reliable sources or a subject expert. Inspect text, anatomy, objects, spatial relationships, historical details, scientific labels, and provenance. Mark uncertainty and revise rather than silently correcting the record.

Known limits

Expect inaccurate labels, impossible geometry, distorted hands or faces, invented sources, style drift, inaccessible contrast, and changing model behaviour. AI output is a creative draft or hypothesis, not proof, consent, attribution, or a substitute for lived experience.

Critical Reflection Questions

Discussion prompts:

  • What evidence supports the factual or contextual claims in an AI-generated image, and which details remain uncertain?
  • Whose perspectives, identities, and experiences are represented or absent, and how might prompt choices reinforce stereotypes?
  • What personal data or third-party works were entered into the tool, and were consent, minimisation, and school procedures followed?
  • Which copyright, licence, attribution, and provider terms apply to the inputs, output, and intended reuse, and why is an AI output not automatically free to use?
  • Which human decisions improved accuracy, representation, and accessibility, and when should a subject expert review the work?
  • When would a non-AI method, a licensed reference image, or no image at all be the more ethical and effective choice?
