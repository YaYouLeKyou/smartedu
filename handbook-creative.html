Creative AI Projects

Image generation, AI music, 3D design, and science-art fusion

In this chapter

AI is not just a tool for analysis and automation — it is a powerful creative partner. This chapter explores how students and educators can use AI for image generation, music composition, 3D design, and the fusion of science and art. Each project encourages students to direct, evaluate, and remix AI outputs rather than accept them passively.

Learning objectives

  • Design and implement AI-assisted creative projects across four domains: image, music, science, and 3D design.
  • Apply critical thinking to evaluate AI-generated outputs for accuracy, bias, and cultural sensitivity.
  • Demonstrate understanding of copyright, licensing, and intellectual property when using AI tools.
  • Adapt projects for diverse learners through differentiated instruction and accessibility alternatives.
  • Create non-digital alternatives to ensure inclusive participation for all students.

1. AI Image Generation in the Classroom

Generative AI image tools let students and teachers create visual content quickly — from concept art to scientific diagrams to historical recreations. But the power of these tools lies not in simply generating images, but in learning to prompt, critique, and remix the outputs.

"The goal is not to replace the artist's eye, but to give every student an imagination amplifier."
4 sessions x 45 min (160 min) Computer/tablet, internet, AI image tool, paper & colours for alternatives Individual or pairs Ages 12+

Measurable Learning Objectives

  • Students will craft effective text prompts to generate at least 3 distinct images aligned with a learning goal (scored rubric: 0-4).
  • Students will critically evaluate 2 AI-generated images for accuracy, bias, and cultural sensitivity (written reflection, 200+ words).
  • Students will produce a final composite artwork combining AI-generated and hand-drawn elements.
  • Students will properly attribute AI tools and source images, demonstrating understanding of copyright (checklist completed).

Materials

  • Computers, tablets, or Chromebooks with internet access
  • Access to an AI image generation tool (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, Leonardo.Ai)
  • Projector or interactive whiteboard for demonstrations
  • Paper, coloured pencils, markers, or paints for hand-drawn alternatives
  • Student reflection journals or worksheets

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Introduction (20 min): Present the concept of AI image generation. Demonstrate how prompts influence output. Discuss what AI "understands" versus what a human artist creates.
  2. Prompt Workshop (30 min): Students write 3 prompts on the same theme (e.g., "a scientist"), varying detail and style. Compare outputs. Discuss how word choice affects results.
  3. Creation Session (40 min): Students generate images for their project topic. They select, refine, and iterate on prompts. They save chosen outputs and note the prompt used for each.
  4. Critical Review (30 min): Students evaluate their own and 2 peers' images using the evaluation rubric: accuracy, bias, cultural sensitivity, creativity. Written feedback provided.
  5. Remix and Finalise (25 min): Students combine AI images with hand-drawn or text elements to create a final piece. They write a 100-word artist statement explaining their creative choices.
  6. Gallery and Reflection (15 min): Students present their work. Class discusses ethical implications and what they learned about AI creativity.

Evaluation Criteria

Rubric (4-point scale: 1=Beginning, 2=Developing, 3=Proficient, 4=Exemplary)

  • Prompt quality: Can the student write specific, effective prompts producing relevant images?
  • Critical evaluation: Does the student identify biases, inaccuracies, and stereotyping in outputs?
  • Creative synthesis: Does the final artwork meaningfully combine AI and human-made elements?
  • Copyright awareness: Does the student correctly attribute sources and understand licensing?
  • Reflection depth: Is the artist statement thoughtful and self-aware?

Differentiation

  • Support: Provide prompt templates with fill-in-the-blank structure. Pair students. Offer a word bank of descriptive terms. Allow verbal explanation instead of written reflection.
  • Extension: Challenge students to generate images from opposing perspectives on a topic, then critique both. Explore advanced prompt techniques (negative prompts, style weights).
  • EAL learners: Provide visual prompt examples. Use image-to-text tools to support vocabulary development. Allow native-language reflections.
Copyright and Licensing Notice: AI-generated images are not automatically free of copyright. Terms of service for each tool differ: some allow commercial use with attribution, others restrict redistribution. Students must check the specific tool's terms of service before using outputs publicly. Even when tools grant usage rights, the AI may have been trained on copyrighted images — this raises unresolved legal questions. Always verify licensing conditions for each tool and each output. When in doubt, treat AI-generated images as requiring attribution and non-commercial use only.
Representation and Bias: AI image generators may reproduce societal biases — underrepresenting certain ethnicities in professional roles, stereotyping genders in occupations, or favouring Western aesthetics. Have students generate images with identity-neutral prompts and compare results across different descriptors. Discuss patterns observed and their implications.

Accessibility and Inclusive Design

  • Ensure AI tools are screen-reader compatible. Provide alt-text descriptions for all generated images.
  • Offer high-contrast mode and adjustable font sizes in the interface.
  • For students with motor impairments, provide voice-to-text prompting options.
  • Ensure all visual content is described verbally during gallery presentations.
Alternative Without AI Tool: Students use magazines, newspapers, and their own drawings to create a collage exploring the same theme. They apply the same evaluation rubric to assess representation, accuracy, and creativity in their handmade visuals. The learning objectives on critical thinking and copyright awareness remain fully achievable through analog methods.

2. AI in Music and Cultural Fusion

AI music tools help students compose, experiment, and blend cultural music traditions. The Smart Edu AI Turkey mobility demonstrated how students created AI-assisted rhythms inspired by scientific themes and cultural elements, producing collaborative digital music projects.

5 sessions x 45 min (225 min) Computer/tablet, internet, AI music tool, speakers, instruments if available Groups of 3-4 Ages 12+

Measurable Learning Objectives

  • Students will compose a 2-minute AI-assisted piece using at least 2 different AI tools, demonstrating understanding of prompt control (rubric: 0-4).
  • Students will identify and discuss at least 2 cultural musical elements incorporated into their composition.
  • Students will evaluate AI-generated music for originality, emotional expression, and cultural authenticity (written review, 250+ words).
  • Students will verify and document the licensing conditions of each AI tool used before publishing outputs (checklist completed).
  • Students will collaborate effectively in international/multicultural teams (peer assessment form).

Materials

  • Computers, tablets, or Chromebooks with internet access
  • Speakers or headphones for audio playback
  • Access to AI music tools (Suno, BandLab, Soundraw)
  • Optional: traditional instruments, microphones
  • Lyric sheets and cultural research materials
  • Student reflection journals

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Cultural Research (30 min): Each group selects a cultural musical tradition to explore (e.g., folk rhythms, classical structures). They research its characteristics, instruments, and cultural significance.
  2. Tool Introduction (25 min): Demonstrate each AI music tool. Students create accounts and explore free features. Introduce basic prompt structures (genre, mood, tempo, instruments).
  3. Composition Session 1 (40 min): Groups use one AI tool to generate a short musical piece inspired by their researched culture. They document the prompt used and the output received.
  4. Composition Session 2 (40 min): Groups use a second AI tool to create a variation. They compare outputs, select the best elements, and combine them manually where possible.
  5. Critical Review (30 min): Groups evaluate their own and 2 other groups' pieces using the evaluation rubric. Written feedback is provided focusing on cultural authenticity and originality.
  6. Presentation and Licensing Check (30 min): Groups present their compositions. Each group presents a licensing verification sheet confirming they checked terms of service for each tool used.

Evaluation Criteria

Rubric (4-point scale: 1=Beginning, 2=Developing, 3=Proficient, 4=Exemplary)

  • Musical quality: Does the composition demonstrate intentional structure, rhythm, and tonal coherence?
  • Cultural integration: Are cultural elements authentically represented rather than superficially copied?
  • Critical evaluation: Can the student identify strengths and weaknesses in AI-generated music?
  • Licensing diligence: Has the student verified and documented the terms of use for each tool?
  • Collaboration: Did all group members contribute meaningfully?

Differentiation

  • Support: Provide prompt templates for music generation (e.g., "Genre: [folk], Mood: [joyful], Tempo: [moderate], Instruments: [drum, flute]"). Offer pre-researched cultural information sheets. Allow oral presentations instead of written reviews.
  • Extension: Explore AI-generated music in contrasting cultural contexts. Analyse how AI handles microtonal scales, non-Western time signatures, or oral traditions. Compose lyrics alongside the music.
  • EAL learners: Provide multilingual research materials. Use visual musical notation. Allow presentation in native language with peer translation.
Copyright and Licensing Verification: Suno, BandLab, and Soundraw are not automatically free of rights. Each tool has distinct terms of service that change over time:
  • Suno: Free tier grants personal, non-commercial use only. Outputs may not be used commercially without a paid plan. Users must verify current terms at suno.com/terms.
  • BandLab: Free tier allows editing and sharing within the platform, but commercial use and distribution require verification of licensing. Check bandlab.com/terms.
  • Soundraw: Generators claim royalty-free output for subscribers, but free-tier users must confirm usage rights per the current license at soundraw.io.
Action required: Before any public sharing or publishing, students must visit each tool's official terms of service page, read the current license, and document their findings. Tool terms change — always verify at the time of use. Outputs from free tiers often carry non-commercial or attribution-only restrictions.
Representation and Bias: AI music tools may underrepresent non-Western scales, instruments, and traditions. They may "flatten" cultural specificity into generic world-music patterns. Have students compare AI outputs for different cultural traditions and discuss representational gaps. Encourage students to supplement AI outputs with authentic cultural research.

Accessibility and Inclusive Design

  • Ensure music tools are keyboard-navigable. Provide visual waveform displays alongside audio.
  • Offer transcript/literal descriptions of musical elements for deaf and hard-of-hearing students.
  • Ensure colour-blind-friendly visual interfaces.
  • Provide alternative non-digital activities: rhythm clapping, body percussion, or acoustic instrument exploration.
Alternative Without AI Tool: Students use body percussion, found sounds, or traditional instruments to create a collaborative composition inspired by their researched cultural tradition. The same evaluation criteria (musical quality, cultural integration, critical review) apply. Students create a licensing awareness poster explaining why they checked copyright for any recorded music they reference.

3. Science and Digital Innovation

The intersection of science and AI opens new frontiers for hands-on learning. From AI-powered microscopy to virtual lab simulations, students can conduct experiments, collect data, and analyse results in ways previously impossible. The Smart Edu AI Turkey mobility showcased AI-enhanced science experiments including VR cell exploration and iNaturalist for biodiversity observation.

4 sessions x 50 min (200 min) Computer/tablet, internet, VR headset (optional), lab equipment, specimen photos Groups of 3-4 Ages 13+

Measurable Learning Objectives

  • Students will conduct at least 1 virtual experiment using an AI-powered simulation, recording hypotheses, methods, and results (lab report scored: 0-4).
  • Students will identify and discuss at least 3 limitations of AI tools in scientific contexts (written analysis, 200+ words).
  • Students will use AI-assisted tools (e.g., iNaturalist) to classify and analyse biological data, achieving at least 80% accuracy against expert benchmarks.
  • Students will produce a science-art fusion piece combining scientific accuracy with creative expression.

Materials

  • Computers, tablets, or Chromebooks with internet access
  • Optional: VR headsets for immersive exploration
  • Access to VR cell exploration platforms, iNaturalist, Khan Academy AI
  • Lab equipment for comparative real-world experiments (microscopes, slides, plant samples)
  • Art supplies for science-art fusion projects
  • Data recording sheets and graph paper

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Hypothesis Formation (20 min): Introduce the scientific question (e.g., "How do cells divide?"). Students form hypotheses and design a basic method. Discuss how AI can support vs. replace hands-on work.
  2. Virtual Experiment (40 min): Students conduct a virtual experiment using AI-powered simulation (e.g., VR cell exploration). They record observations systematically and compare with real-world data where available.
  3. Data Analysis (35 min): Students use AI tools (iNaturalist for species ID, AI data analysis tools) to classify and analyse their data. They compare AI results with manual verification.
  4. Critical Analysis (30 min): Students write a reflection on at least 3 limitations of AI in scientific contexts (e.g., accuracy gaps, missing contextual understanding, data bias). Peer review in pairs.
  5. Science-Art Fusion (40 min): Students create a visual or multimedia piece that accurately represents a scientific concept while demonstrating creative expression. They explain both the science and the art in their choices.
  6. Presentation and Discussion (35 min): Students present findings. Class discusses ethical implications and the role of human judgement in science.

Evaluation Criteria

Rubric (4-point scale: 1=Beginning, 2=Developing, 3=Proficient, 4=Exemplary)

  • Scientific method: Does the student follow the scientific method rigorously, including hypothesis, method, and analysis?
  • Critical awareness: Can the student identify specific limitations of AI tools in their experiment?
  • Data accuracy: Are AI-assisted analyses verified against reliable sources?
  • Science-art integration: Does the fusion piece meaningfully represent both scientific accuracy and creative expression?

Differentiation

  • Support: Provide structured lab report templates. Pair students strategically. Offer pre-selected AI tools with guided tutorials. Allow verbal presentations instead of written reports.
  • Extension: Design a comparative study between AI-assisted and traditional methods. Explore edge cases where AI fails in scientific classification. Publish findings to a class blog.
  • EAL learners: Provide bilingual scientific vocabulary lists. Use visual lab protocols. Allow research in native language with peer translation.
Representation and Bias: AI scientific tools may carry training biases — for example, species databases may be skewed toward Western biodiversities, or medical AI may underperform for underrepresented populations. Discuss with students: whose science is represented in the data? How might AI tools fail in non-Western scientific contexts?

Accessibility and Inclusive Design

  • Ensure VR simulations are accessible (subtitles, audio descriptions, motion sensitivity options).
  • Provide screen-reader compatible data analysis tools.
  • Offer tactile models for visually impaired students alongside virtual explorations.
  • Ensure lab equipment is adaptable for students with physical disabilities.
Alternative Without AI Tool: Students conduct the same experiments using traditional microscopy, physical specimens, and manual classification. They create comparison charts between AI-assisted and manual methods, achieving the same critical analysis and scientific method learning objectives. The science-art fusion project can use hand-drawn or sculptural media.

4. AI 3D Design and Digital Creation

Students create AI-assisted 3D models, digital sculptures, and visual designs reflecting their cultures and scientific themes. This combines technical skill development with cultural expression and scientific inquiry, fostering creativity across multiple domains.

5 sessions x 45 min (225 min) Computer/tablet, internet, 3D software, 3D printer (optional), craft supplies Groups of 3-4 Ages 12+

Measurable Learning Objectives

  • Students will create at least 1 AI-assisted 3D model or design that reflects a cultural or scientific theme (rubric: 0-4).
  • Students will evaluate 2 AI-generated 3D designs for cultural sensitivity and aesthetic value (written review, 200+ words).
  • Students will articulate the relationship between cultural identity and digital design in a 150-word reflection.
  • Students will verify and document the licensing terms for each 3D AI tool used (checklist completed).

Materials

  • Computers, tablets, or Chromebooks with internet access
  • Access to AI 3D design tools (e.g., Runway ML, Canva AI features, DALL-E + 3D tools)
  • Optional: 3D printer for physical output
  • Craft supplies (clay, cardboard, paper) for tactile alternatives
  • Cultural research materials and reference images
  • Student reflection journals

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Cultural Research (25 min): Each group selects a cultural heritage element (architecture, patterns, artefacts) or a scientific concept (DNA, solar system). They research its significance and visual characteristics.
  2. Tool Exploration (20 min): Students explore AI 3D design tools. They test prompts, style settings, and output formats. Document successful and unsuccessful prompts.
  3. Design Session 1 (40 min): Groups create an AI-assisted 3D design or digital model. They iterate on prompts, adjusting style, detail, and cultural references.
  4. Critical Review (30 min): Groups evaluate their own and 2 other groups' designs using the evaluation rubric. Focus on cultural sensitivity, aesthetic quality, and scientific accuracy.
  5. Refinement and Remix (35 min): Students refine designs based on feedback. They manually add or modify elements to ensure cultural authenticity and personal voice.
  6. Presentation and Licensing Check (35 min): Groups present their designs with cultural context. Each group presents a licensing verification sheet.

Evaluation Criteria

Rubric (4-point scale: 1=Beginning, 2=Developing, 3=Proficient, 4=Exemplary)

  • Design quality: Does the 3D design demonstrate technical skill and creative vision?
  • Cultural/scientific depth: Does the design meaningfully engage with the cultural or scientific theme?
  • Critical evaluation: Does the student identify biases or inaccuracies in AI outputs?
  • Licensing diligence: Has the student verified and documented the terms of use for each tool?

Differentiation

  • Support: Provide design templates and example prompts. Pair students with complementary skills. Offer pre-researched cultural/scientific information. Allow 2D design instead of 3D as an alternative.
  • Extension: Explore AI 3D design for architectural planning or scientific modelling. Create animations from static designs. Submit designs to a digital gallery.
  • EAL learners: Provide visual design rubrics. Allow research in native language. Use multilingual 3D design vocabulary cards.
Copyright and Licensing Verification: 3D AI design tools have varying licensing terms:
  • Runway ML: Check current terms at runwayml.com — free tier typically grants limited usage rights; commercial use requires verification.
  • Canva AI: Canva's terms vary by subscription level — verify at canva.com/terms what constitutes acceptable use of AI-generated elements.
  • DALL-E + 3D tools: OpenAI's terms permit use of generated images, but when combined with 3D software, licensing becomes layered and must be verified per component.
Action required: Students must check each tool's current terms of service, document what usage rights they have, and confirm before any public display. Free-tier outputs often carry attribution requirements or non-commercial restrictions only.
Representation and Bias: AI 3D tools may default to Western architectural styles, body proportions, and aesthetic norms. When students generate cultural designs, discuss whether the output reflects authentic representation or a stereotypical interpretation. Encourage students to manually adjust AI outputs for cultural accuracy.

Accessibility and Inclusive Design

  • Ensure 3D software has keyboard navigation and screen-reader support.
  • Provide tactile 3D models (clay, 3D prints) alongside digital designs.
  • Offer high-contrast visual interfaces and adjustable workspace sizes.
  • Ensure VR/AR experiences have accessibility settings (seated mode, reduced motion).
Alternative Without AI Tool: Students sculpt, build, or draw cultural/scientific models using physical materials (clay, cardboard, paper mache). They apply the same evaluation criteria for design quality, cultural depth, and critical reflection. The licensing awareness component shifts to researching and documenting copyright for referenced images and cultural artefacts used as inspiration.
