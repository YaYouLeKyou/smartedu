Chapter 2

AI-Powered Teaching & Learning

Pedagogical strategies for intentional, human-centred AI integration

Back to Handbook

In this chapter

This chapter organises AI usage around pedagogical objectives rather than tools. Each activity is designed around a specific teaching goal — lesson design, critical literacy, assessment, and data-informed discussion — so that AI serves the curriculum rather than driving it. Each section links to a dedicated resource page with learning objectives, classroom activities, and critical reflection points.

Audience K-12 teachers, higher education lecturers, vocational trainers, and instructional designers
Level Intermediate — assumes foundational AI literacy (see Handbook – Foundations)
Duration Self-paced: 60–90 minutes; workshop format: 3 hours across four activities
Prerequisites Completion of Handbook – Foundations or equivalent AI literacy. Familiarity with basic lesson planning.

Measurable learning objectives

  • Design at least one lesson that integrates AI intentionally aligned to a defined learning objective
  • Facilitate a classroom discussion in which students critically evaluate AI outputs using evidence
  • Create an assessment rubric that fairly evaluates AI-assisted student work
  • Interpret learning analytics as discussion indicators, not as labels or predictions about individual learners
  • Justify pedagogical choices about AI use in a written reflection of at least 250 words

Pedagogical objectives in this chapter

  • Design AI-aligned lessons
  • Facilitate critical AI literacy
  • Assess AI-assisted work fairly
  • Use analytics as discussion indicators
  • Critical reflection

1. Pedagogical Objective: Design AI-Aligned Lessons

Effective AI integration begins with the learning objective, not the tool. This activity guides educators through designing a lesson where AI enhances — rather than replaces — pedagogical reasoning.

Activity: Intentional AI Lesson Design

60 minutes Individuals or pairs All subject areas

Materials: Lesson plan template, subject curriculum documents, access to one AI tool of choice, sticky notes or digital board.

  1. Identify objective (10 min): Select one learning objective from your curriculum. Write it in measurable terms (e.g., "Students will compare two historical perspectives using evidence" rather than "Students will learn about history").
  2. Evaluate AI fit (15 min): Determine whether AI genuinely serves this objective or merely digitises an existing task. Use the question: "What can the learner do now that they could not do without AI, and is that gain worth the dependency?"
  3. Design the activity (25 min): Draft a lesson segment where AI plays a defined role — research assistant, draft generator, feedback provider — with clear boundaries on what learners must do independently.
  4. Peer review (10 min): Exchange lesson plans with a partner. Partner evaluates using the checklist: objective clarity, AI role definition, independent work component, and assessment alignment.
Differentiation

Support: Provide a completed example lesson plan showing an AI-aligned lesson vs. a tool-centric lesson. Use a structured template with guiding prompts.

Extension: Design a two-session sequence where AI use increases in complexity across sessions, with explicit reflection after each.

Assessment: Lesson plan evaluated on a 12-point rubric: objective measurability (0–3), pedagogical alignment (0–3), AI role clarity (0–3), and independent thinking component (0–3). Score of 9/12 or above indicates achievement.

2. Pedagogical Objective: Facilitate Critical AI Literacy

Students need to understand AI's strengths and limitations as users, not just consumers. This activity develops classroom facilitation skills for AI literacy discussions.

Activity: Structured AI Output Analysis

45 minutes Small groups (3–4) Secondary / Higher Education

Materials: Printed AI-generated texts or images (2–3 examples on the same topic), analysis worksheet per group, markers, access to verification tools (search engines, fact-checking sites).

  1. Present outputs (5 min): Display three AI-generated texts on the same topic. Ask students: "What do you notice?"
  2. Group analysis (20 min): Each group analyses one text using the worksheet: identify factual claims, assess verifiability, detect bias or omission, and rate confidence in the output (high/medium/low with justification).
  3. Verification (10 min): Groups use search engines and fact-checking tools to verify their assessments.
  4. Class discussion (10 min): Groups share findings. Facilitator guides discussion toward: "When is AI output trustworthy enough to use, and when does it need human verification?"
Differentiation

Support: Provide the analysis worksheet with guided prompts and sentence starters. Pair students strategically.

Extension: Ask students to generate their own AI output on a topic, then write a critical evaluation that could be shared with other students as a model.

Assessment: Review completed worksheets for depth of analysis. Use a rubric: factual claim identification (0–3), verifiability assessment (0–3), bias detection (0–3), and justification quality (0–3). Score of 8/12 or above indicates achievement.

3. Pedagogical Objective: Assess AI-Assisted Work Fairly

When students use AI as part of their workflow, assessment must distinguish between process and product, and between original and AI-generated contributions.

Activity: Assessment Redesign Workshop

40 minutes Small groups (3–4) All levels

Materials: Existing assessment rubric (from your own practice), printed "AI contribution declaration" template, markers.

  1. Review current rubric (10 min): Groups examine their own assessment rubric and identify criteria that could be met equally by AI-generated or human-generated work.
  2. Redesign criteria (15 min): Rewrite criteria to differentiate between process (how the student worked) and product (what was produced). Add criteria for originality and critical engagement.
  3. Declaration design (10 min): Create a brief "AI contribution declaration" template where students disclose how they used AI, what they modified, and what they learned.
  4. Gallery walk (5 min): Groups display redesigned rubrics and declarations; others add sticky-note feedback.
Differentiation

Support: Provide a sample rubric with pre-identified AI-vulnerable criteria. Offer a partially completed declaration template.

Extension: Draft a whole-class policy on AI use in assessments, including provisions for different types of tasks (creative vs. analytical vs. procedural).

Assessment: Groups' redesigned rubrics evaluated on: process-product distinction (0–3), originality criteria (0–3), student disclosure mechanism (0–3), and clarity (0–3). Score of 9/12 or above indicates achievement.

4. Pedagogical Objective: Use Analytics as Discussion Indicators

Important principle: Learning analytics should be treated as indicators for discussion — never as labels, verdicts, or predictions about individual learners. Analytics show patterns in data; they do not define a student's ability, potential, or future outcomes. Always discuss analytics with the learner present, in a collaborative and non-judgemental manner.

Activity: Analytics Dialogue Protocol

30 minutes Pairs (educator-learner role-play) All levels

Materials: Sample analytics dashboard screenshots (anonymised), dialogue protocol cards, reflection journal.

  1. Introduce protocol (5 min): Explain the dialogue protocol: (a) Present data without interpretation, (b) Ask the learner what they notice, (c) Share observations as questions, not conclusions, (d) Co-decide on next steps together.
  2. Role-play (15 min): In pairs, one participant plays a teacher, the other a learner. The "teacher" presents anonymised analytics data using the protocol. The "learner" responds naturally. Switch roles after 7 minutes.
  3. Debrief (10 min): Whole-group discussion: What was difficult about presenting data as questions? When might data be misleading? How does this approach change the power dynamic?
Differentiation

Support: Provide scripted dialogue prompts on the protocol cards. Pair less experienced participants with a facilitator for the role-play.

Extension: Have participants design an analytics-informed learning plan for a hypothetical student, explicitly avoiding any label-based language in their plan.

Assessment: Reflection journal evaluated for: understanding of analytics as indicators (0–4), recognition of limitations and risks (0–4), evidence of critical stance (0–4). Score of 10/12 or above indicates achievement.

Analytics are not verdicts: A dashboard showing low engagement does not mean a student is disengaged — it may reflect the tool's design, the student's access circumstances, or cultural differences in platform use. Analytics should open conversations, not close them. Treat any analytics-based "score" or "risk indicator" as a starting point for dialogue, never as a final judgement.

5. Critical Reflection

After completing the activities, reflect individually or with colleagues:

"What learning objective did I serve by using AI, and would the same objective be achievable without it?"
"When I use analytics dashboards, do I discuss the data with learners, or do I act on it privately?"
"How do I ensure that AI does not narrow the range of perspectives or voices in my classroom?"
"What would I do differently if my institution had no access to this particular AI tool?"
"How do I communicate to students and families that AI outputs require human verification?"

6. Inclusion and Accessibility

AI-enhanced teaching must be designed for all learners from the outset. Key principles for classroom practice:

  • Multi-modal representation: Present AI-generated content in multiple formats (text, audio, visual) so learners with different needs can engage meaningfully.
  • Equitable access: Ensure no learner is disadvantaged by lack of device, internet, or prior AI experience. Provide non-digital alternatives for every AI-enhanced activity.
  • Language support: AI tools should accommodate multilingual classrooms. Verify translation quality before relying on it for important communications. Note: Language-specific practices are covered in the Languages & Communication chapter.
  • Cognitive accessibility: AI interfaces and outputs should be comprehensible at the intended reading level. Avoid tools that increase cognitive load without clear pedagogical benefit.
  • Assistive technology compatibility: Verify that AI tools work with screen readers, captioning tools, and other assistive technologies before adoption.
  • Representation in content: Test whether AI outputs reflect the cultural and social diversity of your learners or default to a narrow perspective.

7. Data Safety and GDPR

When using AI in teaching, educators handle learner data that is protected by law and institutional policy. Key principles:

  • Lawful basis: Ensure a clear legal basis for processing learner data — consent is preferred, especially for minors.
  • Data minimisation: Only share data with AI tools that is strictly necessary for the educational purpose.
  • Transparency to families: Inform parents and guardians about which AI tools are used, what data is processed, and how long it is retained.
  • Learner rights: Learners should know they can refuse AI-assisted activities and request data access or deletion.
  • Security verification: Confirm tools use encryption, access controls, and security certifications before adoption.
  • Third-party chains: Understand whether AI tools share data with subprocessors, and verify downstream protections.

8. Limitations and Human Verification

AI tools in teaching have well-documented limitations that educators must understand and communicate:

  • Accuracy varies: AI outputs can contain factual errors, especially in specialized domains. Verify all outputs before sharing with learners.
  • Bias persists: AI models may produce systematically different quality or perspective for different cultural, linguistic, or demographic contexts. Audit outputs for equity.
  • Context blindness: AI cannot understand individual student histories, classroom dynamics, or institutional culture. Professional judgement remains irreplaceable.
  • Currency limitations: Many models have training data cut-offs. Current events, recent research, and new policy developments may not be reflected.
  • Over-reliance risk: When teachers defer to AI for planning or assessment without verification, errors propagate and professional judgement weakens.
  • Feedback depth: AI-generated feedback often addresses surface features (grammar, structure) but misses deeper conceptual misunderstandings or emotional needs.

Every AI-assisted teaching decision should involve a human review step. This is not optional — it is a core professional responsibility in education.
