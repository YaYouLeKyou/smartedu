What is AI and How is it Used in Education?

A foundational resource for understanding artificial intelligence, its history, and its educational applications

Back to Chapter: Foundations
AI Foundations Beginner 2–3 hours (self-study or classroom)

Resource Overview

This resource traces the evolution of artificial intelligence from early rule-based systems to today's generative models. It provides educators and students with a clear timeline of key milestones and a practical vocabulary to discuss AI confidently. The presentation covers machine learning, natural language processing, computer vision, and large language models, with concrete examples of how these technologies are applied in educational contexts.

The resource was developed collaboratively by the Smart Edu AI partnership as part of the 5-day training program for educators and youth workers held in Strasbourg, France.

"The most important question is not whether AI can do something, but whether it should, and under what conditions."

Audience, Level & Prerequisites

  • Target audience: Teachers, youth workers, students aged 14+, and anyone new to AI.
  • Level: Beginner — no programming or mathematics required.
  • Prerequisites: Curiosity and an open mind. Basic digital literacy (using a web browser, opening files) is helpful but not mandatory.
  • Estimated duration: 90–120 minutes for the core activities; extendable to a 3-hour workshop.

Measurable Learning Objectives

By the end of this resource, learners will be able to:

  • Define artificial intelligence and describe its evolution from rule-based systems to generative AI. (Target: ≥80% accuracy on a 10-item matching quiz.)
  • Distinguish between machine learning, deep learning, and rule-based AI. (Target: correctly classify 4 of 5 scenarios.)
  • Explain how NLP, computer vision, and generative models work in educational contexts. (Target: deliver a one-minute explanation for each.)
  • Identify at least 5 concrete AI applications currently used in education. (Target: list 5 in a group discussion.)
  • Discuss the benefits and limitations of AI tools critically. (Target: contribute at least 2 benefits and 2 limitations in a reflective task.)

Core Concepts

Machine Learning: Algorithms that improve through data and experience without explicit programming — from decision trees to deep neural networks.

Natural Language Processing (NLP): The ability of computers to read, interpret, and generate human language, powering chatbots, translation, and text analysis.

Generative AI: Systems that create new content (text, images, code, audio) based on patterns learned from existing data, including large language models.

Computer Vision: Enables machines to interpret visual information, supporting applications from document scanning to interactive science experiments.

Data-driven decisions: Using learner data to inform teaching, while respecting privacy and consent.

Suggested Classroom Activities

Activity 1: AI Timeline Creation

Duration: 25 minutes  |  Materials: internet access, large paper or digital whiteboard, markers, printed timeline cards.

Steps:

  1. Divide students into groups of 3–4.
  2. Each group researches 3–4 AI milestones from 1950 to the present using credible sources.
  3. Groups place milestones on a shared timeline and explain how each breakthrough enabled new educational applications.

Differentiation: Advanced groups can include speculative future milestones (2030–2040). Support struggling groups by providing a pre-filled milestone list to arrange.

Evaluation: Groups present one milestone; teacher checks accuracy against a rubric (clarity, relevance, sourcing).

Activity 2: Tool Identification Challenge

Duration: 20 minutes  |  Materials: scenario cards, whiteboard, voting stickers.

Steps:

  1. Present students with classroom scenarios (grading, tutoring, content creation, plagiarism detection).
  2. Students identify which AI capabilities are at play and write them on cards.
  3. Class discusses answers, clarifying misconceptions and building vocabulary.

Differentiation: Provide sentence starters for lower-level learners; challenge advanced learners by adding ethical dilemmas to each scenario.

Evaluation: Quick-fire quiz with thumbs-up/thumbs-down responses; track individual participation.

Activity 3: Myth-Busting Workshop

Duration: 30 minutes  |  Materials: myth cards, research devices, "Myth vs. Fact" poster template.

Steps:

  1. Students identify common myths about AI (e.g., "AI can replace teachers", "AI is completely objective").
  2. Groups research each myth using teacher-approved sources.
  3. Groups create a "Myth vs. Fact" poster with evidence and present to the class.

Differentiation: Pre-select myths for struggling groups; encourage advanced groups to investigate bias and fairness claims.

Evaluation: Poster rubric (accuracy of evidence, clarity of argument, teamwork).

Inclusion & Accessibility

  • Use plain language and visual aids (diagrams, icons) to support diverse learners.
  • Provide transcripts for any video content and captions for audio examples.
  • Offer printed handouts for students who prefer offline learning or have limited data connectivity.
  • Ensure activities can be completed individually, in pairs, or in small groups to accommodate different social preferences.

Privacy, Data & GDPR

  • This resource does not require collecting or storing personal student data.
  • If students use AI tools during activities, they must use anonymous or dummy accounts and never enter personal information.
  • Teachers should review the privacy policy of any third-party tool before classroom use and seek parental consent where required.
  • Under GDPR, learners have the right to access, correct, and delete their personal data — schools must ensure compliance when adopting AI platforms.

Limitations & Human Verification

  • AI-generated content is a starting point, not a final authority. All outputs must be verified by the teacher before use in lessons.
  • Large language models can produce plausible-sounding but incorrect or biased information; teach students to cross-check facts.
  • AI tools are trained on existing data and may reflect historical inequalities; critical evaluation is essential.
  • Technical failures, outages, and licensing changes can affect tool availability — always have a non-digital backup plan.

Critical Reflection Questions

Discussion prompts for educators and students:

  • What is the difference between automation and intelligence?
  • Can an AI system truly "understand" content, or is it always pattern-matching?
  • What are three AI tools we use daily that we might not recognize as AI?
  • Who benefits and who might be disadvantaged when AI is introduced into education?
  • How can we teach students to interact with AI tools critically rather than passively?
