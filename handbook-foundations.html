Chapter 1

Foundations of AI in Education

Building shared understanding before exploring tools and classroom practice

Back to Handbook

In this chapter

This chapter establishes the conceptual foundations for understanding and evaluating AI in education. Before introducing any AI tool into the classroom, educators need a shared vocabulary, a clear understanding of what each technology does and does not do, and a critical stance toward claims about personalisation and effectiveness. Each section below links to a dedicated resource page with deeper pedagogical content, learning objectives, classroom activities, and reflection points.

Audience Teachers, instructional designers, school leaders, and education professionals seeking foundational AI literacy
Level Beginner / Foundational — no prior technical knowledge required
Duration 45–60 minutes self-paced; adaptable for a 90-minute workshop
Prerequisites None. Open to all education professionals regardless of technical background

Measurable learning objectives

  • Define AI, machine learning, NLP, computer vision, generative AI, automation, and data in education-specific terms
  • Distinguish between a technology category and its subset or application with at least 80% accuracy in a classification exercise
  • Evaluate at least two claims about AI personalisation using a critical framework
  • Identify at least three data privacy considerations when selecting an AI tool
  • Articulate why human oversight remains essential in every educational AI application

1. Key Concepts: Distinguishing AI Technologies

A common source of confusion in education is the conflation of related but distinct AI concepts. Understanding each term precisely prevents overgeneralisation and supports informed decision-making. The table below clarifies what each concept is, what it is not, and how it appears in educational contexts.

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Is: A broad field of computer science focused on creating systems that simulate human cognitive functions such as reasoning, learning, and problem-solving.

Is not: A single algorithm or a sentient system. AI is an umbrella term covering many approaches.

In education: Adaptive learning platforms, intelligent tutoring systems, and automated grading systems all fall under AI.

Caution: The term is often used in marketing to imply capabilities the system does not possess. Always ask what specific techniques the system uses.

Machine Learning (ML)

Is: A subset of AI in which algorithms learn patterns from data and improve performance with experience, without being explicitly programmed for each task.

Is not: A rule-based system. ML does not follow fixed instructions; it infers patterns from examples.

In education: Recommender systems that suggest learning resources, or models that predict dropout risk based on historical data.

Caution: ML models reflect biases present in training data. Outcomes may not generalise to populations underrepresented in the data.

Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Is: A subset of AI focused on enabling computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language — including text and speech.

Is not: Perfect language understanding. NLP systems process patterns statistically; they do not "comprehend" meaning as humans do.

In education: Grammar checkers, chatbots for practice, automated essay scoring, and translation tools.

Caution: NLP outputs can contain subtle errors, cultural biases, and inappropriate suggestions. Always review before sharing with learners.

Computer Vision (CV)

Is: A subset of AI that enables machines to interpret and analyse visual information — images, videos, and diagrams.

Is not: Human-level visual reasoning. CV systems excel at pattern recognition within trained categories but fail on out-of-distribution inputs.

In education: Document scanning, science experiment analysis, accessibility tools for visual impairments, and diagram recognition.

Caution: CV systems may misclassify or produce false positives, particularly with diverse populations and contexts not represented in training data.

Generative AI

Is: AI systems that create new content — text, images, audio, code — based on patterns learned from existing data, typically using large language models or diffusion models.

Is not: A reliable source of factual information. Generative AI can produce plausible-sounding but incorrect content (hallucinations).

In education: Content creation, lesson planning assistance, creative project support, and brainstorming activities.

Caution: Outputs require verification for accuracy, originality, and cultural sensitivity before use in any educational setting.

Automation

Is: The use of technology to perform repetitive tasks with minimal human intervention — a capability enabled by AI but not equivalent to it.

Is not: A cognitive technology. Automation executes predefined or learned procedures; it does not reason about educational purpose.

In education: Automated attendance tracking, gradebook calculations, scheduling, and notification systems.

Caution: Automating decisions without human review can embed errors and remove professional judgement from critical processes.

Data

Is: The raw material that powers AI systems — learner interactions, assessment results, behavioural patterns, and demographic information collected and processed to train or inform models.

Is not: A technology itself. Data is a resource that requires ethical collection, storage, and use.

In education: Learning analytics dashboards, progress tracking, and personalised pathway recommendations.

Caution: Data collection raises privacy, consent, and equity concerns. GDPR and institutional policies must govern all educational data practices.

Critical note on claims about personalisation

Many AI products assert that they "personalise learning" in a deterministic sense — that the system will reliably match each student to an optimal path. Current evidence does not support this claim unconditionally. Personalisation outcomes depend on data quality, model design, learner engagement, and contextual fit. Treat personalisation claims as hypotheses to be tested in your specific context, not as guaranteed results. Monitor learner progress and gather student feedback to assess whether personalisation features are genuinely beneficial or merely apparent.

2. Structured Learning Activities

The following activities are designed for different learning contexts and provide concrete practice in applying foundational AI concepts. Each activity includes duration, materials, step-by-step guidance, differentiation strategies, and assessment criteria.

Activity 1: AI Concept Mapping and Classification

45 minutes Individuals or pairs Secondary / Higher Education

Materials: Large sheets of paper or whiteboards, coloured markers, printed concept cards (one set per group), printed category labels (AI, ML, NLP, CV, Generative AI, Automation, Data).

  1. Introduction (5 min): Present the seven concept categories and ask participants to predict how they relate to each other.
  2. Sorting task (20 min): In pairs, participants sort concept cards into the correct categories and draw connections between related concepts. They must justify each placement.
  3. Gallery walk (10 min): Pairs visit other groups' maps, adding sticky notes with agreements or challenges.
  4. Debrief (10 min): Facilitator leads a whole-group discussion clarifying any misclassifications and emphasising the distinction between subsets, applications, and enabling technologies.
Differentiation

Support: Provide a reference sheet with definitions and examples for each concept. Pair participants with stronger literacy skills with those who need assistance.

Extension: Ask advanced participants to add a sub-category or identify which concepts appear in a specific AI product, explaining the chain of dependencies.

Assessment: Evaluate each pair's classification accuracy against the reference key. Aim for at least 80% correct classification as the measurable threshold. Collect concept maps for review.

Activity 2: Evaluating Personalisation Claims

40 minutes Small groups (3–4) All levels

Materials: Printed or projected case studies of AI product claims (2–3 examples), evaluation worksheet per group, markers.

  1. Introduction (5 min): Present two conflicting claims about AI personalisation — one from a vendor, one from an independent researcher.
  2. Analysis task (20 min): Groups use a structured worksheet to identify: (a) what evidence is provided, (b) what context the claim applies to, (c) what assumptions are made, (d) what could go wrong.
  3. Group presentations (10 min): Each group presents its analysis in 2 minutes.
  4. Synthesis (5 min): Facilitator summarises common critical lenses and reinforces that personalisation is context-dependent and never guaranteed.
Differentiation

Support: Provide the evaluation worksheet with guided prompts and sentence starters ("The claim assumes…", "Evidence for this would be…").

Extension: Ask groups to design a small-scale test that could verify the personalisation claim in their own institution.

Assessment: Review completed worksheets for depth of analysis. Use a rubric assessing: evidence identification (0–3 points), assumption recognition (0–3 points), risk identification (0–3 points), and clarity of argument (0–3 points). A score of 8/12 or above indicates achievement.

Activity 3: Data Privacy Mini-Audit

30 minutes Individuals All levels

Materials: Printed checklist template, a sample AI tool privacy policy (or a real one from a well-known educational tool), highlighters.

  1. Introduction (5 min): Explain that GDPR requires transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, and consent in educational data processing.
  2. Audit task (18 min): Participants work individually through a privacy checklist, examining a sample tool's policy for: data collection scope, storage location, sharing with third parties, deletion rights, and consent mechanisms.
  3. Peer review (7 min): Participants swap findings with a partner and discuss discrepancies.
Differentiation

Support: Provide the checklist with plain-language explanations for each item and a reference sheet explaining key GDPR terms.

Extension: Ask participants to draft a brief recommendation to a school leader about whether to adopt the tool, citing specific privacy findings.

Assessment: Count the number of correctly identified privacy issues on the checklist (minimum 5 out of 8 for achievement). Review recommendation drafts for specificity and accuracy.

3. Critical Reflection

Before adopting any AI tool, educators should reflect on the following questions individually or with colleagues:

"If this AI tool disappeared tomorrow, would the learning objective still be achievable? If not, is the dependency justified?"
"Whose voices are missing from the data that trains this model, and how might that affect my students?"
"Am I adopting this tool because it genuinely improves learning, or because it is the current trend?"
"How will I verify the accuracy and fairness of this tool's outputs for my specific student population?"
"What will I tell students and families about how their data is used by this tool?"

4. Inclusion and Accessibility

AI tools must be designed and selected with all learners in mind. The following principles should guide practice:

  • Multi-modal access: Ensure tools offer text, audio, and visual output options so learners with different needs can engage meaningfully.
  • Language equity: Tools should support the home languages of learners, not only the language of instruction. Verify translation quality before relying on it for critical communications.
  • Cognitive load: AI interfaces should not overwhelm users with information. Evaluate whether tool outputs are comprehensible at the intended reading level.
  • Device and connectivity independence: Consider how learners without reliable internet or modern devices will access AI-enhanced content. Always provide a non-digital alternative.
  • Disability inclusion: Verify that tools are compatible with screen readers, captioning, and other assistive technologies before adoption.
  • Cultural responsiveness: Test whether AI outputs reflect the cultural contexts of your learners or default to a narrow cultural perspective.

5. Data Safety and GDPR

Educational data is among the most sensitive categories of personal information. The following principles are essential:

  • Lawful basis: Ensure there is a clear legal basis for processing learner data — typically consent or legitimate interest, with consent preferred for minors.
  • Data minimisation: Only collect data that is strictly necessary for the stated educational purpose. Resist vendor pressure to share additional data.
  • Transparency: Learners and families must be informed about what data is collected, how it is used, how long it is stored, and who has access.
  • Right to access and deletion: Learners and families should be able to request a copy of their data or its deletion, in accordance with GDPR Articles 15 and 17.
  • Security measures: Verify that tools use encryption, access controls, and regular security audits. Do not accept tools that store data without clear security certifications.
  • Third-party sharing: Understand whether learner data is shared with subprocessors or other third parties, and verify that adequate protections exist downstream.
  • Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA): For new AI tools processing sensitive data at scale, conduct a DPIA before deployment, involving relevant stakeholders.

6. Limitations and Human Verification

Every AI system has limitations that educators must understand and communicate. Key limitations include:

  • Accuracy gaps: AI outputs can contain errors, especially in domain-specific or edge-case scenarios. Always verify before using in assessment or feedback.
  • Bias and fairness: Models may produce systematically different outcomes for different demographic groups. Audit outputs for equity, not just accuracy.
  • Context blindness: AI lacks the contextual understanding that experienced educators bring — knowledge of individual student histories, classroom dynamics, and institutional culture.
  • Temporal currency: Many models are trained on data with a cut-off date. Current events, emerging research, and recent policy changes may not be reflected.
  • Over-reliance risk: When educators defer to AI outputs without verification, errors propagate and professional judgement atrophies.
  • Emotional and social dimensions: AI cannot genuinely understand or respond to emotions, motivation, or social dynamics in the way a human educator can.

Human verification is not optional — it is a core professional responsibility. Every AI-assisted decision in education should involve a human review step, documented and accountable.
